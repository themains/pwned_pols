\begin{abstract}
\noindent Data breaches involving politicians are concerning because of the threat of impersonation, blackmail, and other nefarious activities. To shed light on the concern, we estimate how frequently politicians' email addresses are compromised. Using a dataset of 12,385 email addresses from politicians across 59 countries spanning three decades, we check whether these addresses are part of breaches using Have I Been Pwned, a widely used online service for searching public breach data. A third of the observed addresses appear in at least one public breach, and over one in five appear in a breach exposing sensitive data such as bank account numbers, biometric data, browsing history, chat logs, or credit card CVV. Decomposing appearances by how the data was exposed shows that most are not compromises of accounts politicians held: 23.5\% of addresses appear in broker or scrape aggregation, against 15.9\% in a breach of a service the politician plausibly used and 2.32\% in corpora derived from infostealer malware. Benchmarked against a general-population sample on a shared catalogue and exposure window, politician addresses appear substantially less often, though differences in breach-corpus coverage leave that comparison unidentified. These are lower bounds: we do not observe every address used by each politician, and HIBP covers only public indexed breaches.
\\

\noindent
\textbf{Keywords:} Cybersecurity, Have I Been Pwned (HIBP), Elites, Data breaches, Security and privacy
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} 
Data breaches can reveal login credentials, personal details, and service usage patterns, enabling impersonation, extortion, and blackmail. When breaches involve politicians, the stakes are higher \citep{dnc2016hack_guardian, twitterhack2020, twitterhackwitman2022}. These compromised credentials can give adversaries access to private communications. Such access can be weaponized to influence elections, as in the 2016 DNC hack and the 2017 Macron campaign breach \citep{dnc2016hack_guardian, dnc2016hack_crowdstrike, macron2017hack, shires2019}. They can also be used for blackmail or to impersonate prominent officials, as in the 2020 Twitter hijackings of Biden and Obama \citep{twitterhack2020, twitterhackwitman2022}. Even seemingly innocuous data from old breaches of defunct accounts, like passwords and contact information, enable credential-stuffing attacks on unrelated platforms, which can again expose politicians \citep{pal2022might, OWASPCredStuff, li2019protocols, gabbard2025wired}. Hence, unlike breaches of ordinary citizens, which primarily harm only the individual, breaches of politicians threaten democratic governance \citep{shires2019}.

Data breaches are also exceedingly common \citep{sood2019pwned, mayer2021angry, Sood_Have_I_Been_2023}. A study of a large random sample of Americans found that more than 80\% of people have had their data breached \citep{sood2019pwned}.\footnote{A follow-up study using the Florida voter registration data found a similar percentage of people had had their data breached \citep{Sood_Have_I_Been_2023}.} While some of the breaches are innocuous, involving already public information, many of the breaches involve private data. For instance, nearly 72\% of the public breaches include passwords. Even when only login credentials are compromised, the threat is sizable. Because many people reuse their passwords or only make minor tweaks, attackers using credential tweaking attacks can compromise 83\% of the accounts \citep{li2019protocols} (see also \cite{Chintalapati_Pass-Fail_Using_a_2022}). The threat is nearly as grave even when leaks involve hashed passwords. Attackers can use resources like \url{hashes.org}, which contains clear text values for passwords to crack passwords. In 2021, a study found that nearly 99\% of the hashed passwords in the \textit{Have I Been Pwned} (HIBP) database can be recovered \citep{kanta2021viable}.
These estimates, however, come from the general population rather than elected officials and political elites. They also sit well above the rates we report below, and we take that contrast up directly: \cref{sec:sm} compares our sample against \citet{sood2019pwned} on a shared breach catalogue and a shared exposure window, where politician addresses appear in breaches considerably less often than those of US adults. We caution there that uneven national coverage of breach corpora leaves that difference unidentified.

This paper studies the prevalence of politician email-address breaches. We assemble a large dataset of addresses spanning 59 countries over three decades and check them against HIBP. Nearly one in three observed addresses have had at least one breach, and more than one in five have had a ``serious'' breach, in which private data such as credit-card information may be exposed. These address-level rates are lower bounds rather than population-level estimates for politicians.

Two further results bear directly on how those rates should be read, and we develop both in the Supplementary Information. First, exposure is dominated by third-party aggregation rather than by compromise of politicians' own accounts: more addresses appear in broker and scrape corpora than in breaches of services the politician plausibly used. Second, on a shared catalogue and a shared exposure window, politician addresses appear in breaches far less often than addresses in a general-population sample. Neither result is a clean estimate of relative risk, because coverage of breach corpora varies by country and address type. Both, however, argue against reading the headline rate as a measure of how often politicians' accounts are compromised.

\section{Research Design, Data, and Measures}
\label{sec:data}

Our estimand of interest is the frequency with which politicians' data, especially sensitive data, is breached. Our population of interest is all politicians who have held a legislative or executive role at a national, state, or local level in the last three decades. Our definition of population excludes politicians who have never served the government. 

To study the question, we assemble a large, diverse convenience sample. Our final data set has thousands of politicians from 59 countries spanning three decades.

To measure how often politicians' data is leaked, we check public breach data using politicians' emails. We do this because email addresses allow us to confidently connect a breach to a person. The downside is that our method only enables us to estimate a conservative lower bound of the frequency with which data are leaked for three reasons. First, the breached data must have email addresses. Some public breach data may not; for instance, it may only have usernames and passwords (which can, based on other breaches, be connected back to the politician). Second, we need all the email addresses used by the politician to know all the public data breaches in which a politician's data has been exposed. We only have official email addresses for many of the politicians. There is also reason to expect that exposure estimated using official email addresses is lower than estimated using personal emails. We expect politicians to be less likely to use official emails when opening online accounts, as the official email accounts for politicians (in many Democracies) are likely temporary. We also expect politicians to be reluctant to associate their official emails with private activities because of disclosure laws and official policies restricting the use of official emails. Lastly, not all breaches become public.

Our data on data breaches comes from HIBP, which collates data from more than 850 public breaches and classifies breached data into nearly 150 categories. Some data breaches are benign, revealing mainly public information; others are more serious, revealing sensitive data. To better understand the actual threat posed to politicians, we classify breaches as serious based on the kind of data breached. Our measure leaves out the most serious breaches. HIBP doesn't provide data on sensitive breaches---breaches that can be embarrassing, e.g., adult websites like Adult FriendFinder (2015), Ashley Madison, etc.---via its public API. It only allows people who can verify the emails to see those breaches \citep{HIBP}.

In all, we start by providing a conservative lower bound of the threat faced by politicians. Next, to account for the attenuating bias stemming from private vs. official emails, we craft a measure distinguishing the two and model the probability of being connected to a data breach. The model yields an adjusted association between address type and exposure rather than a corrected prevalence; we report it as such. We also describe the variation in threat across politicians based on country and gender. Lastly, we analyze how politicians' data comes to be leaked. We examine which breaches are responsible for the leaks.

\subsection{Data on Politicians}\label{sec:emails}
Our data on politicians comes from two sources. The first is \href{EveryPolitician.org}{https://Everypolitician.org/} \citep{mysociety2018}. The data has been used extensively by researchers \citep[see, for e.g.,][]{eshima2022just, stockemer2018age, martini2024, Kosinski2024} and as a source for building other databases \citep{kurz2023introducing, stockemer2022introducing}. The data covers legislatures from across the world between 1997 to 2019. 

From the data, we exclude any legislature with fewer than 30 politicians with a listed email address. In all, we have 8,538 unique email addresses from 61 legislatures across 55 countries.

We supplement this data with data gathered by scraping official parliamentary sites of the Argentinian, Brazilian, Danish, Greek, Indian, Nigerian, Norwegian, and Singaporean parliaments (or Senates) and the Indian state legislatures of Bihar, Delhi, Himachal Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, and Uttar Pradesh. We scraped the data in January 2025.

We collate the two datasets and check if the email addresses conform to expected email formats (e.g., RFC 5322-compliant addresses like \textit{name@domain.com}). To check, we first preprocess email strings by stripping whitespace and normalizing character representation by converting them to Unicode \citep{pev}. Next, we check if the domain exists via DNS resolution and check deliverability by looking up DNS MX records. Emails that fail these checks are removed from the data because querying HIBP with an invalid email (e.g., \textit{an@invalid.email}) will result in a ``No breach found'' status, biasing our estimates downwards.

Our final dataset has 12,385 unique politician email accounts from 67 legislatures across 59 countries from 1997 to 2025, representing close to 50\% of the global population living under electoral regimes \citep{RoW, owid} and 36\% of the global population overall. \cref{tab:tableone} summarizes our data.

\subsection{Official Vs. Personal Emails}

We classified an address as \emph{official} if its domain was government‑controlled (e.g., ccTLDs and SLDs such as \texttt{.gov}, \texttt{.gouv}, \texttt{.gob}, \texttt{admin.ch}, \texttt{nic.in}) or contained clear institutional tokens (e.g., ``parliament,'' ``senate,'' ``congress,'' ``assembly,'' including common non‑English variants). We classified an address as \emph{personal} if the domain was a major webmail provider (e.g., Gmail/Yahoo), a non‑government commercial domain (e.g., \texttt{.com}, \texttt{.net}, \texttt{.biz}), or a campaign/staff‑managed domain lacking government indicators. The full pattern lists are in \cref{tab:regex_core_government_domains,tab:regex_political_parties_organizations,tab:regex_commercial_personal_domains,tab:regex_known_parliaments_ministries}.

Two caveats motivate robustness checks. First, some ``official'' mailboxes can be role accounts shared by staff; conversely, some personal domains are professionally managed. Second, exposure risk can correlate with the propensity to publish a personal address. We therefore report a role-account sensitivity analysis using transparent local-part prefixes and alternative seriousness thresholds in \cref{tab:serious-sensitivity}. A within-politician comparison would settle this, and it is not available here, though the obstacle is narrower than the absence of any identifier. EveryPolitician assigns an identifier to each of 16,351 person-legislature records and supplies a Wikidata identifier, which resolves to the individual rather than to the seat, for 11,369 distinct people; 68 records share a Wikidata identifier because the same person served in two legislatures. What is missing is a second address. Keyed on the Wikidata identifier, 6,173 people have an observed address and exactly one has two, both of them institutional. The comparison is therefore blocked by address coverage rather than by linkage, and would be identified by collecting one additional address per politician against the Wikidata key. Decade fixed effects are included where legislative start years are available.

\subsection{Measuring Exposure to Data Breaches}\label{sec:hibp-data}
To evaluate whether politicians' data has been breached, we check emails against the \textit{Have I Been Pwned} (HIBP) database. Researchers frequently use HIBP to study breaches \citep[see, for, e.g.,][]{hien2024discovering, sood2019pwned, Sood_Have_I_Been_2023}. For each breach, HIBP reports the type of data exposed, for example, email addresses, hashed passwords, sexual orientation, email messages, etc., and includes metadata such as the year of the breach, its scope (number of records affected), and the industry of the breached online service. HIBP covers breaches dating back to 2007, though publicly available records begin in 2013 (\cref{tab:hibp_breach_summary,fig:breach_summary-years_until_breach_known-breach_size}).

\subsection{Serious Breaches}\label{sec:serious-breaches}

\paragraph{What makes a breach ``serious''?}
We considered a breached data class \emph{serious} when exposure plausibly causes (i) direct account compromise or recovery abuse across services (a security harm), or (ii) durable, hard‑to‑reverse risks such as identity theft, financial loss, coercion, discrimination, or persistent tracking (a privacy/pecuniary harm). This aligns with widely used guidance that distinguishes basic identifiers from sensitive PII and ``special‑category'' personal data \citep{NIST800122, GDPRArticle9}, recognizes that even apparently low‑sensitivity fields can become sensitive when linked (the ``mosaic effect'') \citep{OMB_M1313, NIST800122}, and treats device/online identifiers as personal data when linkable to a person \citep{GDPRRecital30, ICOOnlineIdentifiers}. In the account‑compromise channel, exposure of credentials (including recoverable hashed passwords) or recovery factors materially lowers the work factor for \emph{credential stuffing} and related attacks; modern authenticator guidance therefore mandates screening new passwords against known‑compromised sets and discourages knowledge‑based questions \citep{NIST80063B, NIST80063FAQ, OWASPCredStuff, kanta2021viable}.

\paragraph{Measurement boundary.}
\textit{Have I Been Pwned} (HIBP) aggregates public breach corpora and labels each incident with the types of data exposed. However, the public API suppresses some of the most sensitive incidents unless the querier can verify control of the inbox/domain (e.g., certain adult or stigmatizing sites). Consequently, our ``serious'' counts are \emph{lower bounds} even conditional on observing an address.

\paragraph{Operational categorization.}
HIBP assigns 146 data classes. We grouped them into three mutually exclusive tiers based on dominant harm mechanism and reversibility:

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item \textbf{Tier~1 (Profile/Contact).} Names, usernames, email addresses, phone numbers, job titles, IPs, and similar profile attributes. Primary risks are targeting and nuisance; harms are generally indirect and more reversible.

\item \textbf{Tier~2 (Credential/Account).} Passwords (hashed or clear), password hints, auth tokens, historical passwords, login histories, security Q\&A, PINs. Primary risk is cross‑site account takeover via credential stuffing or recovery abuse; harms are immediate but usually remediable by rotation/invalidations, with residual risk from unknown reuse \citep{NIST80063B,OWASPCredStuff,kanta2021viable}.

\item \textbf{Tier~3 (Highly Sensitive).} Financial identifiers (credit cards, CVV, bank accounts), government IDs/passports, biometrics, health/insurance, private messages/email content, browsing histories, photos with sensitive context, SSNs/tax IDs, and similar special‑category data. Primary risks are direct fraud, identity takeover, profiling/coercion, and long‑term stigma; many elements are non‑revocable (IDs/biometrics; leaked communications) and thus create persistent exposure \citep{NIST800122, GDPRArticle9, FTC2025Fraud, NISTBiometricsPrivacy, ICOSpecialCategory}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Country characteristics}
\label{sec:country-level-data}
To investigate cross-country variation in exposure, we collect five country-level predictors.
To measure a country's level of digitization, we collect the UN e-Government Development Index (EGDI), a composite of government online services, telecommunication infrastructure, and human capital \citep{undesa2020, qog2026}.
Regime type comes from the V-Dem electoral democracy index over the same window \citep{vdem2026}. For national cybersecurity effort, we use the 2020 edition of the Global Cybersecurity Index (GCI), a 0--100 score of national cybersecurity commitments \citep{itu2021gci}.
We code whether English is an official language, taken from the CEPII GeoDist database \citep{mayer2011geodist}.
Finally, we collect countries' GDP per capita \citep{wb}.
Where multi-year data exist, we take the average over 2010 to 2020---for digitization this covers the five biennial editions of the survey published between 2012 and 2020.
The UN indices cover member states only, which excludes several small territories (Bermuda, French Polynesia, Greenland, Guernsey, Hong Kong, and Jersey), and Andorra lacks a V-Dem score, giving \CrossCountryN\ countries in the final analytical sample.


\section{Results}
\subsection{Prevalence of Politician Email Breaches}
Nearly one in three observed email addresses is linked to at least one public breach (Panel A, \cref{tab:pooled_pols_breach_summary}). This pools incidents that differ in what they imply about the account holder, and \cref{tab:breach_taxonomy} decomposes it: 23.5\% of addresses appear only because a data broker or scraper held them, while 15.9\% appear in a breach of a service the politician plausibly held an account with. Rates below should be read as exposure of the address, not as compromise of the politician's accounts. However, there is a sharp skew in the distribution. While the mean number of breaches per account is 1 ($\hat{\sigma} = 3.2$), the median is 0, with 19.5\% of accounts breached multiple times, some as many as hundreds of times.

Serious data breaches were associated with 21.6\% of observed email addresses (Panel B, \cref{tab:pooled_pols_breach_summary}). Of the breached addresses, the table reports the corresponding serious-breach share. Again, we see a substantial skew. Most addresses are never seriously breached, but a smaller proportion are breached multiple times.

The most common data exposed in breaches involving politicians are email addresses, names, phone numbers, job titles, and physical addresses (\cref{tab:hibp_pwnedpols_datatype}). While these are mostly innocuous data, passwords are the most common serious data class exposed. More than 63\% of breached addresses had a password-related class exposed; this denominator is breached addresses, not all observed addresses. In nearly 1.6\% of the account breaches, credit card information was revealed. Bank account numbers were compromised in 1.0\% of the breaches, and government-issued IDs in 1.3\%. Some breaches also exposed private communications, such as emails (2.3\%) and other private messages (0.7\%).

\subsection{Official Vs. Personal Emails}
\cref{tab:pooled_pols_breach_summary}'s numbers paint too rosy a picture. As we note above, for many politicians, we only have their official government email addresses. To shed light on the issue, we analyze how the number of breaches varies by official vs. personal emails.

\cref{tab:pooled_pols_breach_summary} tabulates the number of breaches by personal and official email accounts. Of the 12,385 observed addresses, 3,014 are personal. A personal address is almost twice as likely as an official one to appear in at least one breach (\cref{tab:pooled_pols_breach_summary}). The breach rate for official emails is 28.4\% vs. 47.3\% for personal emails (Panel A, \cref{tab:pooled_pols_breach_summary}). The relative difference for serious data breaches is more pronounced, with the respective numbers being 17.2\% and 35.2\% (Panel B, \cref{tab:pooled_pols_breach_summary}). It is tempting to read the personal-address rate as the truer measure of a politician's exposure, but two considerations argue against doing so directly. The next subsection shows the difference may partly reflect selection into publishing a personal address, and the provenance decomposition in \cref{tab:breach_taxonomy} shows that much of the official-address exposure is broker aggregation of addresses published by design rather than compromise of accounts.

\subsection{Who Gets Breached?}
The personal vs. official email results may be driven by selection bias. Politicians who use personal email providers may be more lax about their security or anticipate shorter political stints. To better address the confounds and to study the variation in exposure more generally, we model breaches as a function of politician characteristics. Given the skew in the data (\cref{tab:pooled_pols_breach_summary}), we model the likelihood of an email breach rather than the number of breaches. In all, we estimate the following equation:
\begin{multline}
\label{eq:breach_lpm}
\text{Breach}_{i,t} \;=\; 
\beta_1\,\text{PersonalEmail}_i
\;+\; \beta_2\,\text{Female}_i 
\;+\; \beta_3\,\text{SocialMedia}_i \\
\;+\; \gamma_{c(i)} 
\;+\; \tau_t
\;+\; \theta_i
\;+\; \lambda_{c(i), i}
\;+\; \varepsilon_{i,t},
\end{multline}

where $i$ indexes addresses, $c$ indexes country, and $t$ indexes the decade of the legislative start year. The data are cross-sectional, with one observation per address; $t$ enters only through decade fixed effects. Our base specification models the risk of a breach (or serious breach) as a function of email type (personal vs. official), country, and decade-fixed effects (based on the legislative start year). For the EveryPolitician data, we have more variables available. We take advantage of the richer data by adding controls for politician gender ($\beta_2$), whether or not the politician has a known Twitter or Facebook account ($\beta_3$), legislature type (lower house, upper house, unicameral legislature, $\lambda_{c(i), i}$), and political party ($\theta_i$). Since we expect observations within the same country to share unobserved characteristics that may induce correlation in the error terms, we cluster the standard errors at the country level. Because the design has only 59 country clusters and one country supplies 26.9\% of addresses, we also report null-imposed WCR11 wild-cluster p-values with 9,999 Rademacher draws as a small-cluster diagnostic. We estimate the models using \textit{fixest} in R \citep{fixest}.

Before turning to the results, a necessary caveat: because of potential confounds, cross-group differences cannot be interpreted as stemming from some essential aspect of the group. For instance, one of the many potential confounds that could vitiate comparisons is that the risk of exposure is correlated with political tenure. If, for instance, male legislators may have longer tenures than female legislators, the coefficients on gender will partly reflect differences in tenure (which is not modeled).

Personal emails have a 24--30 percentage points greater chance of having a breach (serious breach) associated with them ($p < .001$, \cref{tab:breach_lpm} Columns (1)--(4)). \section{Discussion}
Data breaches expose people to theft, extortion, impersonation, and blackmail. When the target is a politician, the social cost of a breach is likely considerably higher. Politicians handle sensitive government information, influence policy, and engage with influential figures. Their public statements shape discourse. The corresponding risks from impersonation, blackmail, etc., are hence greater \citep[for example,][]{dnc2016hack_guardian, macron2017hack, germany2019, twitterhack2020, twitterhackwitman2022}, and compound the substantial cybersecurity gaps documented in government infrastructure worldwide \citep{Ribeiro2025GIQ}. The threat is amplified when we account for systematic efforts to exploit information or election interference, misinformation campaigns, and undermining of democracy \citep{dnc2016hack_guardian, dnc2016hack_crowdstrike, macron2017hack, uk2017breach, germany2019, shires2019, norway2020, finland2020, twitterhack2020, tomz2020}.

In this study, we assemble a large database of politician email addresses to assess exposure in public breach corpora. Conservatively, more than one in five observed addresses have had sensitive data breached at least once. Because addresses are an incomplete proxy for people and HIBP is incomplete, this should not be read as a population-level rate for politicians.

The composition of that exposure matters as much as its level. Most of it arises because a third party held the address rather than because an account was compromised, and only 2.32\% of addresses appear in corpora derived from infostealer malware, the one channel that indicates a compromised device. Benchmarked against a general-population sample on a shared catalogue and window, politician addresses appear in breaches several times less often, not more. We do not read that as evidence that politicians are safer: their published addresses are institutional and comparatively recent, and the samples differ in national coverage of breach corpora. We do read it as evidence that a bare prevalence figure, uncompared and undecomposed, cannot support claims about how exposed political elites are relative to anyone else.

There is a sharp skew in exposure, with a small proportion of addresses repeatedly compromised. This pattern could arise from several reasons, including credential reuse, weak passwords, and attacks targeting certain politicians \citep{dnc2016hack_guardian, macron2017hack, germany2019}.

Correspondingly, there is also a sharp skew in the number of politicians entangled in each breach. A few large breaches account for most of the exposure. This pattern mirrors that of the general population \citep{sood2019pwned}. Part of the pattern is founded in leaks from a few widely used online services, such as LinkedIn and Dropbox.

Prior studies of data breaches have focused on the general population. Using the same HIBP database, a study of a representative sample of 5000 American adults found breach rates of 80\% \citep{sood2019pwned}, a smaller online survey of 413 participants found a breach rate of 73\% \citep{mayer2021angry}, and one using over a million Florida registered voters found 84\% \citep{Sood_Have_I_Been_2023}. In comparison, our estimated prevalence of serious breaches of politicians exceeds 50\%. These numbers suggest that politicians are not much better protected than the public despite the higher stakes of compromise.
The harm does not stop with the individual politician either.
Breached data feeds hack-and-leak operations \citep{shires2019}.
And in survey experiments, people who learn that an election was interfered with come away trusting democracy less \citep{tomz2020}.
The breached politician data we document here plausibly lowers the cost of mounting such compromising operations, at least by reducing the effort needed to obtain compromising material in the first place.



\subsection{Real-world breaches}
Political data breaches, though rarely documented systematically \citep{dunncavelty2020}, as in this study, are far from hypothetical.
Recent years have seen many high-profile incidents of breaches that vividly illustrate the potential fallout when elected officials are compromised \citep{dnc2016hack_guardian, dnc2016hack_crowdstrike, macron2017hack, uk2017breach, germany2019, norway2020, finland2020, twitterhack2020, twitterhackwitman2022}.

For instance, in the lead-up to the 2016 US presidential election, Russian-affiliated actors compromised the email systems of the Democratic National Committee and senior Democrats.
The leaked emails were widely disseminated online and fueled disinformation campaigns and political attacks, undermining public trust in a deeply polarized election \citep{dnc2016hack_guardian, dnc2016hack_crowdstrike}.
A year later, in a parallel case, Emmanuel Macron's presidential campaign was hacked just days before the French runoff vote.
Thousands of authentic internal emails and documents, along with fabricated materials, were leaked in an apparent attempt to compromise his candidacy \citep{macron2017hack, shires2019}.
In 2019, personal data belonging to hundreds of German politicians, including Chancellor Angela Merkel and others from major political parties, was leaked, exposing contact details, private chats, ID documents, and credit card details \citep{germany2019}.
%https://www.theguardian.com/world/2019/jan/04/german-politicians-personal-data-hacked-and-posted-online
In 2020, attackers successfully accessed Twitter's internal tools through social engineering, hijacking the verified accounts of prominent figures such as Barack Obama, Joe Biden, and Michael Bloomberg, among others.
The breach was used to promote a Bitcoin scam that netted over US\$100,000 in cryptocurrency before getting shut down \citep{twitterhack2020, twitterhackwitman2022}.
More recently, in 2025, it was revealed that the US Director of National Intelligence, Tulsi Gabbard, had repeatedly reused the same weak password across multiple personal accounts, raising concerns about the cybersecurity practices of top intelligence officials \citep{gabbard2025wired}.


\subsection{Implications for practice}
These incidents, along with the broader patterns we document, highlight the need for stronger safeguards surrounding public officials and political digital infrastructure \citep{Ribeiro2025GIQ}.
While some breaches involved sophisticated tactics in attacking third-party services \citep{twitterhack2020, twitterhackwitman2022}, many arguably stem from preventable vulnerabilities, such as weak passwords, credential reuse, and the absence of two-factor authentication \citep{consolvo2021democracy, germany2019,germany2019-weak-passwords,twitterhack2020, gabbard2025wired, hegseth2025nyt, Ribeiro2025GIQ}.
% \cite{germany2019} https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-46757009-----suggests password reuse---"A cyber analyst told the BBC there was speculation that hackers might have exploited weaknesses in email software to get hold of passwords that those targeted had also used on social media accounts."
Interviews with US campaign staff confirm that political campaigns routinely lack dedicated IT support, rely on personal devices and accounts for sensitive work, and adopt security measures on an ad-hoc basis \citep{consolvo2021democracy}.
At a minimum, governments and political organizations should institutionalize baseline levels of cybersecurity hygiene.

Before listing those levers, we note what they cannot reach. The provenance decomposition in \cref{tab:breach_taxonomy} shows that the single largest channel of exposure is commercial aggregation: addresses harvested into broker, scrape and enrichment databases that the politician never transacted with. Password managers and multi-factor authentication do nothing about this. Reducing it requires limiting the bulk publication and resale of official contact details, which is a procurement and data-protection question rather than a security-hygiene one. The levers below therefore target the service-compromise and malware channels, which are smaller but are where individual and institutional practice binds.

Given our measurement scope, the most effective levers align with the Tier~2/3 risk channels (credential compromise and exposure of highly sensitive attributes): (i) \emph{Eliminate credential reuse} via mandatory password managers and screening new passwords against known‑compromised sets; pair breach notifications with just‑in‑time prompts, which have been shown to increase password changes \citep{albayram2024investigating}, and use password meters as a visual aid \citep{pw_strength_meter}; (ii) \emph{Harden authentication} by enforcing phishing‑resistant multi‑factor authentication on official domains (e.g., security keys/platform authenticators); as precedent, the UK Parliament tightened authentication controls after the 2017 incident \citep{uk2017breach, uk2017breach_update}; (iii) \emph{Monitor and respond at the domain boundary}: centrally track verified government domains against breach corpora, and when an official address appears in a newly added incident, notify the holder, rotate credentials, and revoke third‑party tokens relying on that address; (iv) \emph{Make the secure action the default}: deliver short, role‑specific training embedded in these workflows rather than standalone awareness courses. These steps target the concrete downstream risks we observe (credential‑stuffing, high‑fidelity targeting) without over‑inferring national‑security impacts we do not measure.

\subsection{Limitations}
First, addresses are a lower‑bound instrument: we miss cases where politicians use unobserved personal addresses, and HIBP suppresses certain sensitive incidents via its public API. Second, some official boxes are role accounts; some personal/campaign domains have professional IT support—classification errors would attenuate differences. Third, not all breaches are public; lags between breach and public availability (median $\approx$ 0.5 years) bias us toward undercounting recent exposure. Fourth, we do not measure compromise of classified networks; our findings concern public breach corpora tied to the address. Fifth, cross‑sectional comparisons (e.g., gender or country FE) may reflect correlated behaviors such as tenure, media visibility, or differential address publication rather than inherent susceptibility. Finally, the provenance classification is our own and rests on breach descriptions rather than on forensic evidence of how each corpus was assembled; several large corpora explicitly mix stealer output with recycled credential lists, so the boundary between the combolist and service classes is not sharp. The population benchmark is subject to a stronger caveat still: it compares samples drawn from different countries, and because national representation in breach corpora is uneven, the level difference cannot be attributed to differences in behavior or security practice.

\subsection{Conclusion}
In summary, politicians' contact data circulates widely in public breach corpora, and the sensitive-data share is large enough to warrant stronger security practices for public officials. But the decomposition above should redirect where that effort goes. The largest single channel is not weak passwords; it is commercial aggregation of official contact details, which no amount of individual hygiene addresses and which is a matter for procurement and data-protection enforcement. The credential-hygiene measures we recommend remain justified, and they target the smaller service-compromise and malware channels where they can actually bind.

\subsection*{Breach provenance}

The headline breach rate pools incidents that differ in what they imply about the account holder. We therefore assign every breach in the catalogue to exactly one of three classes according to how the data was exposed. \emph{Broker / scrape aggregation} covers data brokers, scrapers and enrichment services: the address was held by a third party the politician had no relationship with. \emph{Credential combolist} covers aggregated credential dumps, spam corpora and stealer logs, whose constituent sources are mixed or unknown. \emph{Service compromise} covers breaches of a service the account holder plausibly held an account with, and is the only class that supports a statement about a politician's own accounts.

Assignment is rule-based on HIBP's \texttt{IsSpamList} flag and the text of the breach description. Four of the 857 breaches are assigned by hand where the rules misclassify them; the overrides and their written rationales ship in the replication package.

\cref{tab:breach_taxonomy} reports the result. Of the 33.0\% of addresses that appear in at least one breach, 15.9\% appear in a service compromise, 23.5\% in broker or scrape aggregation, and 15.7\% in a credential combolist. These shares do not sum to 33.0\% because an address can appear in more than one class.

The implication is that the headline rate substantially overstates how often politicians' own accounts have been compromised. More addresses are exposed because a commercial aggregator held them than because a service the politician used was breached, and an address can enter a broker database through no action of the politician at all. This also helps explain the official/personal gradient reported in the main text: official addresses are published by design, which is precisely what makes them attractive to harvest.

\subsection*{Comparison with population samples}

To place the level of exposure in context we compare against \citet{sood2019pwned}, a survey of 5,000 US adults whose addresses were queried against HIBP in 2018. Three harmonisations are required before the samples are comparable.

First, the catalogue. The two studies queried HIBP at different dates, against catalogues of 293 and 857 breaches respectively, and the smaller is a strict subset of the larger. We restrict both samples to the 293 breaches they share.

Second, the exposure window. The common catalogue spans 2007--2018, so restricting to it also bounds the period over which exposure could have occurred. Because politician addresses are comparatively recent, we further restrict them to addresses whose earliest observed legislative term began in 2018 or before, leaving 8,488 addresses.

Third, address type. Most politician addresses in our sample are institutional, whereas survey respondents supplied personal addresses. We therefore also report politicians restricted to personal-domain addresses.

\cref{tab:three_sample} reports all three. Under the fullest set of restrictions, 17.6\% of politician addresses and 24.6\% of politician personal addresses appear in at least one of the common breaches, against 82.8\% of US adults.

We stress that this is a benchmark and not a control. US addresses are over-represented in HIBP's corpora, and our sample contains no US legislators, so the difference in levels is confounded by coverage and is not an estimate of relative risk.

Subject to that caveat, the comparison does not support the view that politicians are unusually exposed. The direction is the opposite, and it survives the two adjustments available to us: matching the exposure window raises the politician rate only from 15.3\% to 17.6\%, and restricting to personal addresses raises it to 24.6\%, still well below the benchmark. The composition differs as sharply as the level: 71.7\% of US adults appear in a service compromise against 7.7\% of politicians, so the general-population sample is exposed largely through accounts its members held, and ours largely through addresses others published. We draw no conclusion about relative risk from this. We do conclude that a prevalence figure of this kind carries no evidential weight until it is compared against something, and that reporting one unanchored invites the reader to supply a comparison that the data does not support.

\subsection*{Stealer-log exposure}

HIBP loads a small number of infostealer and malware corpora into the ordinary breach dataset. Several originate in documented law-enforcement operations: the \textit{Emotet} data was supplied following the FBI and Dutch NHTCU takedown, and the \textit{Qakbot} data following the US Department of Justice operation. These differ from the rest of the catalogue in that appearance indicates credentials captured from an infected machine rather than a third party holding the address.

\cref{tab:stealer_logs} reports exposure across the nine such corpora. 287 addresses, or 2.32\% of the sample, appear in at least one. Of those, 216 appear in one corpus, 40 in two, 25 in three and 6 in four. The rate is 5.54\% among personal addresses and 1.28\% among official addresses.

These figures are lower bounds even by the standards of the rest of this paper. HIBP maintains a separate stealer-log dataset, larger than the portion loaded as breaches, which is queryable only by parties who have verified control of the address or of its email domain. We can therefore observe whether an address appears in the corpora loaded as breaches, but not the websites against which the credentials were captured. The corpora we do observe also differ in provenance: those supplied after law-enforcement operations are curated victim lists, whereas those collated from criminal distribution channels are bulk dumps, and it is the latter that account for nearly all of the appearances we record. An infection whose logs were never dumped publicly is invisible to us.

This channel is small but is the one that speaks most directly to the security of politicians themselves, because appearance indicates credential capture from a compromised device rather than a third party holding the address. Two features are worth noting. Repeat appearance is common: 71 of the 287 exposed addresses appear in more than one corpus, which is difficult to explain as incidental inclusion. And the rate is roughly four times higher for personal than for official addresses, though this may reflect which address is entered into a browser on a personal device rather than differential infection of politicians' machines. We also note that credentials captured this way typically include session cookies, which authenticate without invoking a second factor; this is the specific threat that motivates our recommendation of phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication rather than one-time codes.

\subsection*{Sensitivity to construction choices}\label{sec:sensitivity}

Two steps in building these figures are judgement rather than arithmetic. We report what each costs rather than asserting that either is innocuous.

The first is the classification of four breaches by hand, where the rules described above misassign them; those four carry roughly 2.7\% of all appearances. \cref{tab:sensitivity_taxonomy} reverts each in turn to the class the rules would have given, and then all four together. No share moves by more than 1.07 percentage points under any specification, and the movement is concentrated almost entirely in one breach (\textit{DemandScience}). Broker aggregation exceeds service compromise in every specification, including the one that reverts all four hand-coded rows; the replication package fails the build if that ordering ever ceases to hold.

The second is the rule for collapsing addresses observed in both sources, which affects 531 of the 12,385 addresses. \cref{tab:sensitivity_dedup} varies it. Taking the maximum, the sum, or the first of an address's breach counts gives identical results, because the duplicate rows are structured so that one carries every breach and the other carries none; only taking the minimum differs, and it selects the row that observed no breach data. The choice of legislative start year changes how many addresses fall inside a given exposure window, but leaves every rate unchanged. The seriousness threshold, the third such choice, is reported separately in \cref{tab:serious-sensitivity}.
